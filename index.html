Pressing the demo song

First, every downbeat

A neural beat tracker, Beat This!, marks each beat. Jumps may only land on the first beat of a bar, and the meter comes from what it hears. Four beats to a bar is never assumed.

Every bar meets every other bar

Harmony and timbre become a fingerprint for each bar. In this map, a pale line is a stretch of music that comes back. The demo song was measured just now, in your browser.

The neighbours have to agree

One bar can match by luck. So four bars before the seam and four after must match too, and the two places must sit at least 16 bars apart.

Then a finger moves the record

The jump waits for the downbeat, and the two bars overlap for 30 ms. A bad jump breaks all of it: no match, no downbeat, no overlap.

Songs join the same way

A portal needs tempos within 5%, the same chords for four bars on both sides, and no sung line across the seam. The owner’s 518 songs hold 2,341 of them.

Ears have the last word

No change to a jump goes in until it has been rated blind, against what it would replace. Two cleverer joins, a volume dip and a bass swap, sounded worse, so they’re gone.

One song, every groove

The versions of a song share one record, a ring each. Vinyl is the owner’s favourite, and the phone makes it live from any song.

Under your thumb

On the bike, the phone stays in a bag with the screen off. Three buttons on a Cardo helmet intercom run everything, with a beep or a spoken word back. Try them here.

  • ×1Pause or resume
  • ×2Vinyl on or off
  • ×3Next station
  • ×1Next portal
  • ×2A new song now
  • ×1Restart, or the song before
  • ×2Forever on or off

 

It needs no laptop, just more patience

A laptop analyses best. For a song you just downloaded, a small beat tracker runs on the phone: a 3:33 song took 17.5 seconds, and 62% of its jumps matched the laptop’s. Playback is always offline.
